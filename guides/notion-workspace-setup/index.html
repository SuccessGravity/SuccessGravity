📚 How to Set Up a Notion Workspace for Your Business

By David · Founder & Editor, Success Gravity · ✓ Last updated: October 2026 · Pricing verified

Notion is one of those tools that looks deceptively simple when you first open it — a blank page with a flashing cursor — and deceptively powerful once you understand how it's structured. The gap between "I signed up for Notion but can't figure out what to do with it" and "Notion replaced three of our tools" usually comes down to one thing: whether you set up the workspace with intention before adding content.

This guide covers the five setup steps that turn a Notion workspace from an empty canvas into a working business operating system: getting the workspace and team structure right first, building databases for projects and tasks, and using Notion's templates and AI assistant to reduce the ongoing effort. The goal isn't to build the most elaborate workspace — it's to build one your team will actually use consistently. According to Notion's own help center, the Plus plan (around $10/member/month) is where teams unlock unlimited guests and extended version history, but the steps below work on the free plan too.

Notion workspace hierarchy: one workspace containing multiple teamspaces, each with its own pages and databases.

Step 1: Create Your Workspace and Invite Your Team

When you sign up at notion.so, Notion creates your first workspace automatically. Give it a clear name — your company name or project name — since you can have multiple workspaces and you'll want to distinguish them at a glance. A workspace is the top-level container: everything you create in Notion lives inside it.

To invite team members, go to Settings & Members → Members and add them by email address. Notion's free plan allows you to add up to 10 guests with limited page access; the Plus plan removes that restriction and lets every team member access all shared teamspaces. Set roles carefully: Full Access lets members create and edit anything; Can Comment limits them to commenting on pages; Can View is read-only. For most small teams, Full Access for core members and Can Comment for stakeholders or clients works well.

Before you add any content, decide on your workspace structure. The most common mistake is diving straight into creating pages without a plan, ending up with dozens of top-level pages that are hard to navigate. The steps that follow will help you avoid that.

Step 2: Set Up Your Teamspaces

Teamspaces are the major sections of your workspace — think of them as departments or functional areas. Notion's Plus plan and above supports full teamspace control; on the free plan, the sidebar serves a similar organizational role. Common teamspace names for small businesses include: Projects, Operations, Marketing, Finance, HR / People, and Customer Success.

Keep the number of top-level teamspaces small — three to five is usually right for teams under 20 people. Each teamspace gets its own access settings, so your Finance teamspace can be restricted to the finance team while Projects is visible to everyone. Create a teamspace by clicking the + next to "Teamspaces" in the left sidebar, give it a name and icon, and set the access permissions before adding any content.

Inside each teamspace, create a Home page as the first page — a simple overview that explains what lives in this teamspace and links to its main databases. This makes onboarding new team members significantly faster, since there's always a logical starting point.

Step 3: Build Your First Project Database

A database is where Notion's real power starts. Inside your Projects teamspace, create a new page and choose Table as the view type — this creates a database. Name it "Projects." Each row is a project; each column is a property. The standard properties to add at minimum are: Status (Select: Not Started / In Progress / Done / On Hold), Owner (Person: assigns a team member), Due Date (Date), and Priority (Select: High / Medium / Low).

Once you've added properties, create additional views of the same database by clicking + Add a view at the top. A Board view groups projects by Status and gives you a Kanban-style overview — useful for weekly stand-ups. A Calendar view shows projects by Due Date and is helpful for spotting deadline clusters. All views show the same data; switching between them is just changing how you look at it. Notion's help documentation covers all available view types and their configuration options.

Add a filter for each view where it helps — for example, a Board view filtered by Owner = Me shows only your own projects. Saved filters mean the view is always meaningful without manual adjustment each time you open it.

Step 4: Set Up Task Management with Connected Databases

Projects and tasks are most useful as two separate but connected databases. Create a second database named "Tasks" inside the same Projects teamspace. Add properties: Task name, Status, Assignee (Person), Due Date, and — crucially — a Relation property that links each task to a row in the Projects database.

The Relation property is what makes Notion's databases genuinely powerful for project management. Once you've linked Tasks to Projects, you can open any project and see all its related tasks in a Rollup — a count of total tasks, done tasks, or the nearest due date — without leaving the project's page. This connected-database approach is what lets Notion replace a dedicated project management tool: the Projects database gives the high-level view; the Tasks database handles the granular work.

For recurring tasks — weekly reports, monthly reviews, client check-ins — use Notion's Templates feature inside the Tasks database to create a pre-filled task row with standard properties already set. Creating a new recurring task then takes a single click rather than re-entering the same fields each time.

Step 5: Use Templates and the Notion AI Assistant

Notion's built-in template gallery (accessible via the Templates button in the left sidebar) includes hundreds of pre-built pages and databases for common business workflows — meeting notes, CRM pipelines, editorial calendars, OKR trackers, employee onboarding, and more. These templates are a significant time-saver for teams setting up their workspace: instead of designing a meeting notes database from scratch, import a template and customize the properties to match your team's language and workflow.

Notion AI (available as an add-on at around $8 to $10 per member per month, per Notion's pricing page) integrates directly into the editing experience. Highlight any block and you can ask the AI to summarize it, improve the writing, translate it, or generate a first draft of a related document. For knowledge management — turning a rough meeting transcript into a clean summary, or expanding bullet points into a full SOP — Notion AI reduces the friction of documentation enough that teams are more likely to keep their knowledge base current. It's not a replacement for structured setup, but it meaningfully lowers the cost of keeping your workspace organized.

💡 David's tip: Build a "linked database" view of your Tasks database inside each project page using /linked view of database. Filter it automatically to show only tasks where Relation = this project. Every project page then has a live task list without maintaining a separate list — the database stays as the single source of truth. This is the one Notion mechanic that most new users miss, and it's the one that makes the whole system feel effortless once it's in place.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Notion free for small businesses?

Notion's free plan is generous — it includes unlimited pages and blocks for individuals, and collaborative blocks for small teams (up to 10 guests). The free plan covers basic databases, task management, note-taking, and document sharing. The Plus plan at around $10 per month per member unlocks unlimited guests, version history beyond 7 days, and larger file uploads, which is where most growing teams will eventually land. For a solo founder or two-person team, the free plan is often sufficient to start.

Can Notion replace tools like Asana or Trello?

Notion can replace Asana or Trello for many teams, but it requires intentional setup. Out of the box, Notion is a blank canvas — you build the project management structure yourself using databases, views, and properties. Asana and Trello provide structured task management immediately. Notion's advantage is flexibility: once configured, a Notion workspace can combine your CRM, knowledge base, project tracker, and meeting notes in one place — something purpose-built PM tools cannot do. Teams that invest in setup often find it replaces multiple tools simultaneously.

What is the difference between Notion pages and databases?

Pages are documents — text, images, embeds, and sub-pages. Databases are structured collections of pages where each item (row) has defined properties like Status, Date, Owner, or Tag, and can be viewed as a Table, Board, Calendar, Gallery, or List. For project and task management, databases are the core building block. Most well-structured Notion workspaces use databases for anything that needs to be tracked, filtered, or sorted, and pages for written content like meeting notes, SOPs, and documentation.

How do I get my team to actually use Notion?

Adoption usually follows the work, not the other way around. Start by moving one real workflow your team already runs — weekly meetings, project tracking, or client onboarding — into Notion, and build a template for it that eliminates manual setup. Make Notion the single source of truth for that workflow and remove the old tool. Once people experience the reduction in friction, adoption of the next workflow becomes easier. Notion's own template gallery has pre-built templates for most common team workflows that reduce the initial setup effort.
